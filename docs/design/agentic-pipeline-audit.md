# Forge pays for relay, not judgement

Forge's pipeline is not too thorough where the question expects it to be: the planner and spec-writer together are **13.7% of agent spend** ($259 of $1,888 across 144 jobs), and the review panel's one lens that actually blocks, `security`, earns its $126 by forcing 33 of the 63 rounds any lens forced and tagging 8 of the 10 blockers ever written. The waste sits elsewhere. **Build is 57% of spend, 17 points of it rework rounds**; 80 of 97 round-2+ panel runs re-confirm a pass (~$130); the approver parks 56 jobs of which 47 are later approved as-is, mostly by an agent stand-in; and an estimated **10–12% of total spend (~$235) goes to orchestrator sessions** whose own transcripts show they relay `forge job advance` output rather than decide anything, because `step()` already decides. The inbox, office and per-job sessions are one prompt in three lanes; the office (the foreman) has decided **0 of 22 gates** on this repo. The outside evidence of October 2026 points the same way: a single agent over a deterministic harness, one clean-context reviewer with bounded rework (two to three rounds), an approver that is policy rather than a model, code that owns the loop. On config, `.forge/config.yml` has grown to **149 leaf keys (+73% documented keys in seven days)**, of which about 36 need to stay structured; the rest derive from the repo, belong in a preset, are prose, or are dead. The highest-value changes, in order: move the orchestration loop into the pool and demote the three LLM lanes; make rework rounds diff-scoped and cap them at two before escalating; turn the approver into a CLI gate; fold the planner into the spec-writer; shrink config behind `extends:` and inference. One caveat governs everything below: Forge has run **no A/B eval on any pipeline variant**, every dollar is an API-list-equivalent from `lib/pricing.json`, not a bill, and the spec-approval statistics are mostly an agent approving an agent. Where the evidence is thin, the text says so.

## How to read this report

Each section states **Forge today** (facts from code and the 148 job directories, cited `file:line` or by the `forge stats --json` field), **Outside evidence** (cited to the source; vendor claims are marked), an **Inference** (my reading, kept apart), a **Recommendation**, its **Expected effect**, and **How to measure** it with Forge's own instruments: `forge stats`, `forge eval start --set <key>=<value>` A/B overlays (`skills/forge/reference/evals.md`), and config overrides on live jobs. Forge data is at commit `40c1c4f6` (2026-10-19): 148 job dirs, 144 non-eval jobs, 131 with usage, 1,777 agent runs, median job $10.30 and 43.8 agent-minutes, median 103 minutes to PR. Orchestrator-session costs are from the 85 daemon logs under `.forge/runtime/daemons/`, priced per deduplicated assistant message; they under-count output tokens (the AGENTS.md transcript gotcha) and are a floor. ## Question 1: build, rework and review, not planning, carry 80% of the bill

The stage table (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:102-103`, rules in `lib/step.ts:36-70`) is `plan → spec → design → build → verify → review → integrate → decide → pr`, with intake, verify, panel selection, verdicts, integrate and PR handling in code and everything else a sub-agent. Measured over 131 jobs with usage, spend splits as **build round 1 40.4%, review 23.0%, rework builds 17.0%, spec 11.0%, approver 4.0%, plan 2.7%, integrator 1.7%** (`forge stats --json` by role; the round-1 vs rework split of the builder's 57.5% came from an ad-hoc script not kept in the repo). Rounds are the lever: a one-round job costs a median $7.17, two rounds $12.05, three rounds roughly $19–22. Before deciding what to cut, it helps to know what the literature says about pipelines like this at all. The most controlled 2026 measurement, 260 configurations across five architectures, finds every multi-agent arrangement *loses* 2–15% on SWE-bench Verified against a single agent (0.522 single vs 0.506 centralized), at 1.6–6× the turns and as little as a third of the success per 1K tokens (centralized), because frontier single-agent baselines above ~45% leave no room for coordination gains ([arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v3)). Tokenomics puts **59.4% of a role pipeline's tokens in code review** ([arXiv 2601.14470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.14470)). Forge's own split (review + rework = 40%) is milder than that but points the same way: the refinement loop, not generation, is where the money goes.

### Planner and spec-writer should be one step

**Forge today.** The planner (opus, `agents/planner.md`) runs under `pipeline.plan: auto` when the request exceeds 600 chars or has three list lines (`PLAN_AUTO_CHARS/LINES`, `lib/step.ts:75-76`). It ran on 109 jobs: **104 `PLAN: SINGLE`, 5 `PLAN: SPLIT`**, for $51 total (median $0.41, 0.8 agent-minutes, 1.5 wall-clock minutes). Its only output that changes the pipeline is the `PLAN:` line (`parsePlan`, `lib/step.ts:210`); `Size` and `Services` are re-read from `spec.md`, not `plan.md` (`lib/step.ts:947-954`). The spec-writer receives `plan.md` as a path only (`lib/brief.ts:158-169`), reads the codebase itself, and produces a median 24,414-char spec against the planner's 3,486-char plan; 95 of 104 specs reproduce the plan's `## Phases` as `### Phase` headings. In effect the planner is a $0.47-per-job scoping pass whose decision fired five times, at ~$10 per split decision, plus one orchestrator turn and one hand-off gap (median 82 s, p90 315 s per hop, memory `pipeline-speed-analysis-2026-10-14.md`).

**Outside evidence.** None of the products surveyed here runs a separate planner *and* a separate spec-writer: Claude Code plan mode, Jules and Spec Kit all produce one plan artifact from the agent that then builds ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices); [github/spec-kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit)). The only controlled spec-first measurement attributes its small gain (+0.15 on a 5-point judge, +3%) to *repository-grounding hooks*, not to the spec document ([arXiv 2604.05278](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05278)). Positive planner ablations exist only on weak baselines (AgentForge: GPT-4o at 14% on SWE-bench Lite, [arXiv 2604.13120](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13120v1)). In strong SWE-bench systems "planning" is sometimes a cheap workflow selector, not a long-form planning agent ([arXiv 2506.17208](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17208v1)).

**Inference.** The planner's grounding value is already delivered by the spec-writer, which reads more of the code; its residual value is the split verdict, which the spec-writer can emit as a header line and which `step` can read where it reads `PLAN:` today. What would move: the `split-approval` park (P3) becomes a second outcome of the spec stage, so `parseSpecHeader` (`lib/step.ts:223`) needs the verdict and SP5 must tell `split-approval` from `spec-approval`; `splitMode: never` moves into the spec-writer's brief; a SPLIT is decided after a ~$1.4 spec of the umbrella instead of a $0.41 plan (five times in 109 jobs, a few dollars total).

**Recommendation.** Merge the roles: the spec-writer owns `PLAN: SINGLE|SPLIT` in `spec.md`'s header and stops after the `## Jobs` list on SPLIT; delete `pipeline.plan: auto|always` or keep `always` only as an explicit opt-in for very large requests. **Expected effect:** about $0.47 and 2–4 minutes of wall-clock per job (one agent, one hand-off gap, one orchestrator turn), 3–4% of spend, no quality change the literature would predict. The dollar saving is small; the point is one fewer role, prompt hash and roster row to maintain. **How to measure:** `forge eval start --set pipeline.plan=never` against the default on the six current eval tasks; watch `avgRounds`, `firstPassRate`, `avgCost`, `avgMinutes`, and that split counts on live jobs do not fall below the historical 5/109. Evidence strength: moderate (consistent vendor practice, no frontier-model role ablation).

### The spec gate is a human gate humans rarely use as one

**Forge today.** `pipeline.specApproval: always` parked 125 jobs; the result was **128 `approve-spec` to 4 `revise-spec`** (`specFirstApprovalRate` 0.968). Median wait 1.4 minutes, mean 15.5, because most approvals came from a supervising Claude session acting as the owner's stand-in ("Owner-delegated approval", "stand-in owner (overnight run)"); `human` events do not distinguish a person from the stand-in (`actor.source` is `gh/git/os` for both). About 70 of 128 approvals carried a substantive note (beyond "approved from dashboard"), often a builder hint ("new tests often fail only on CI's Windows leg"). The designer never ran (`pipeline.design: none`) although 41 specs said `Needs design: yes`, so the spec-writer's design flag is uncalibrated for this repo.

**Outside evidence.** Humans reject **39% of plans** presented for approval in Claude Code but only 3% of individual permission prompts, and human vigilance on repeated prompts decays from ~17% to ~5% block rate over a session ([Anthropic, auto mode](https://claude.com/blog/auto-mode-default-in-claude-code)). Every vendor with a gate puts it on the plan and the PR (Jules editable plan, Spec Kit "review each result", Copilot refinement before PR) and none publishes a size or ambiguity threshold ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/coding-agent/about-coding-agent); [MLMastery on Jules](https://machinelearningmastery.com/practical-agentic-coding-with-google-jules/)). The HITL literature's advice is to route by risk and track override rate as a health metric, since declining override rates are "a leading indicator of rubber stamping" ([tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-15-human-in-the-loop-rubber-stamp)).

**Inference.** The plan-level gate is where a *human* adds the most signal, so the right move is not to remove it but to stop spending it on jobs nobody reads. A 3% revise rate on this repo is either a very good spec-writer or an agent approving an agent; the data cannot tell, which is itself the finding.

**Recommendation.** Set `pipeline.specApproval: large` as the default and gate `Size: S/M` specs only when a rule fires (a reserved topic, `Needs design: yes`, a protected path in the Approach, a request with a question in it). Record who decided: add an `actor.kind: person|stand-in|foreman` to the `human` event so `specFirstApprovalRate` becomes measurable. Keep the note channel; it is the cheapest builder hint Forge has. **Expected effect:** ~100 fewer parks per 125 jobs, a median 1.4 and mean 15.5 minutes of wait removed each, one orchestrator resume turn each (~$0.3–2.7 session-reported, job 274 turn 3). **How to measure:** `humanReworks` and `avgRounds` for S/M jobs gated vs ungated; the person-only revise rate once the actor kind exists. Evidence strength: thin on *when* to gate; strong that the gate's human signal is real when a human reads it.

### The panel is worth keeping; what it blocks on is not

**Forge today.** `forge job panel` picks lenses deterministically from `review.panel` by `always`, spec suggestion, `paths` globs over the diff and `carried` (`choosePanel`, `lib/step.ts:298-362`); reviewers run in parallel as one `spawns` batch (RV4, `lib/step.ts:1093`), each a fresh context reading the diff; the CLI, not the reviewer, turns `[blocker]/[major]` tags into the verdict against `review.blockOn` (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:360`). That design is the one with published positive numbers. Per lens over all rounds (reviews, rates and USD from `forge stats --json`; blockers counted from `- [blocker]` lines in the review files, because `stats.reviewers.<lens>.blockers` reads 0 for every lens):

| Lens | Reviews | CHANGES rate | Blockers | Rounds it alone forced | USD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| security (opus) | 164 | 0.262 | 8 | **33** | 125.83 |
| code | 215 | 0.065 | 1 | 5 | 102.07 |
| docs | 191 | 0.037 | 0 | 4 | 58.56 |
| design (opus, since 10-15) | 73 | 0.110 | 0 | 6 | 43.41 |
| tests, ui, ux, accessibility (retired 10-15) | 225 | 0.022–0.093 | 1 | 1 | 103.71 |

Security's findings cluster in the same hole families (git output reshaped by repo config, option injection, unchecked paths, ReDoS: `.forge/jobs/*/review/round-*/security.md`, retro P1 in `forge-retro-2026-10-13-spw1/retro.md`). Of 72 jobs that ran a design-family lens, 61 passed (~$110). `stats.reviewers` records dispute rates of 0.225 for `code` and 0.25 for `design`, and 244 `recurringFindings` (same lens and path raised in several rounds). Since the 10-15 simplification, `code` and `security` change rates held (0.025, 0.262) while `docs` (0.043) and `design` (0.095) stayed low. Verifiers, the haiku yes/no checks that run between verify and the panel (`lib/verifiers.ts`, VF1–VF3), are **configured 0 times**.

**Outside evidence.** Independent, context-isolated reviews *aggregated into one report* beat a single pass (SWR-Bench: ten merged samples +44% F1, [arXiv 2509.01494](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.01494)); persona labels add nothing measurable (MARS, [arXiv 2509.20502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20502)); a second model adds false positives rather than true positives ([arXiv 2606.15689](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15689v1)); cross-model review paid off in one direction only (Claude reviewing Codex, not the reverse) ([arXiv 2607.21656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21656)). Devin Review's clean-context reviewer finds ~2 bugs per PR, 58% severe (vendor, [Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)). Watson Labs' ~12,000 review verdicts with seven parallel lanes report that without per-lane exclusion lists "you get the same finding seven times", that lanes return pass/warn/fail with only fail blocking, and that 29% of issues never achieved a clean pass and shipped on warn ([Watson Labs](https://blog.watson-labs.co.uk/6-learnings-from-12000-agentic-code-reviews/)). Anthropic's internal policy blocks merge, among findings, only on open *bug or security* ones, drops style and cleanup findings, and adds a vote stage where "a separate group votes on each finding" before posting (vendor, [claude.dev](https://claude.dev/blog/solving-code-review-at-anthropic/)). The practitioner merge-gate rule is that a finding class may block only when it is objectively decidable, cheap to verify and measured at ≥90% precision, with same-day demotion on one confirmed false block ([matthewswong.com](https://www.matthewswong.com/en/blog/ai-code-review-merge-gate-ci/)).

**Inference.** Forge's panel matches the rewarded shape (independent contexts, deterministic merge of severity tags) and its data matches the literature: the lens that blocks is the one looking for bugs and security holes; `docs` and `design` never produced a blocker in 264 reviews and their majors are themes and copy. Running them is cheap insurance for a repo whose PRs nobody edits after merge (`humanDeltaRate` 0.009); letting them *block* a rework round is not supported by anything.

**Recommendation.** Keep the panel. Add a per-lens `blocking: false` (warn lane) and set it on `docs` and `design`: their findings land in the PR body and `job findings`, not in a new round. Put `design` on sonnet unless a job's spec says `Needs design: yes`. Add a verifier for the recurring security families (`--` before revisions, `O_NOFOLLOW`, regex quantifiers) scoped to `lib/**`: it runs on haiku at roughly $0.01 before the panel and sends the builder back without spawning four reviewers. Consider a cheap falsification pass on `[blocker]`/`[major]` findings before they force a round, with the evidence rule the Adversarial Review paper found necessary (a finding may be dropped only against a cited diff line; 0.457→0.533 F1, [arXiv 2608.18167](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18167v1)). **Expected effect:** the 10 rounds `docs`/`design` alone forced disappear (each a median $4.5–5 rework build plus a panel); the design lens drops from $0.55 to ~$0.2 per run; the verifier catches a share of the 33 security-only rounds at a hundredth of the cost. **How to measure:** `reviewers.<lens>.changeRate` and `soleChanges` (not yet a stat; derivable from `job.verdicts`), `avgRounds`, and whether `humanDeltaRate` or post-merge revert commits move off zero when `docs`/`design` stop blocking. Evidence strength: moderate for warn lanes and dedup; thin for per-lens precision (no study breaks out non-code lanes).

### Rework rounds should be diff-scoped and stop at two

**Forge today.** `pipeline.maxRounds: 3`; 64 jobs (52%) passed in one round, 36 took two, 17 three, 7 four, with 4 `rounds-exhausted` parks of which 3 were approved with findings open. Round-1 verdict sets said CHANGES 39% of the time; rounds 2+ said CHANGES 18% (13 in round 2, 2 in round 3, 2 in round 4), and **80 of 97 round-2+ panel runs passed, ~$130 of the $158 round-2+ review spend**. A rework round re-spawns the *whole carried panel* (RV9 carries, never drops, `lib/step.ts:1081`) although `review.onPrFeedback: changed` already re-runs only `always` lenses after PR feedback (`roundLenses`, `lib/step.ts:258-266`). The builder in a rework round is fresh, gets only the non-PASS review files, findings first (`lib/brief.ts:172-195`). Half of the extra rounds come from security findings in the families above.

**Outside evidence.** Two self-repair rounds capture **76–95% of achievable gain** across seven models ([arXiv 2604.10508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10508v1)); about **59% of what Anthropic's reviewer kept flagging** was code unchanged since its first pass; it moved to diff-only re-review after the first few passes, never re-posts a finding, and after three rounds asks the author to reconsider the approach; its first-round pass rate went 65%→78% and ≥5-round PRs 8.9%→1.5% (vendor, [claude.dev](https://claude.dev/blog/solving-code-review-at-anthropic/)). Watson Labs saw per-round pass rate halve after round 2 and fixes cause 218 new fails in previously clean lanes ([Watson Labs](https://blog.watson-labs.co.uk/6-learnings-from-12000-agentic-code-reviews/)). Stripe caps CI retries at two before a human takes over (secondary, [paperclipped](https://www.paperclipped.de/en/blog/stripe-minions-autonomous-coding-agents)); Bugbot Autofix stops at three ([cursor.com/docs/bugbot](https://cursor.com/docs/bugbot)).

**Inference.** Forge's round-3 yield (2 CHANGES in 17 panels) is the literature's plateau. The 244 recurring findings and the 4 blockers tagged in rounds 2+ say a lens that passed can be broken by the fix, so the `always` lens must re-run; the rest need not.

**Recommendation.** On a rework round, re-run only the lenses that said CHANGES plus `always` lenses, brief them with the previous round's findings and the diff *since that round*, and tell them not to re-raise a finding already recorded. Set `pipeline.maxRounds: 2`; round 3 becomes `rounds-exhausted` → escalation, which is what Anthropic's "step back" is. **Expected effect:** round-2+ review spend roughly halves (~$80 of $158 in this sample), 17 three-round jobs and 7 four-round jobs park a round earlier (each round ~$5 build + ~$2.4 review), total ≈ 5–7% of spend, and the human sees the stuck jobs sooner. Risk: slightly more escalations; the 3 rounds-exhausted jobs approved with findings open suggest humans already ship on warn. **How to measure:** `--set pipeline.maxRounds=2` A/B on evals (`avgRounds`, `escalationRate`, `checkPassRate`); on live jobs, `recurringFindings` and blockers found in round 2+ by a lens that was not re-run (it should be zero; if not, widen the rule). Evidence strength: strong on round count; moderate on lens subset.

### The approver is 70% rule-reproducible

**Forge today.** The approver (opus, $0.53 median) ran 133 times: **77 APPROVE, 56 ESCALATE** (0.369). Of 50 escalated jobs, 47 were later approved as-is, 54 approvals by the stand-in with a one-line reason. Keyword classes over the 56 reasons: permissions/auth/daemon trust 29, runbook human steps 16, CI config path 12, reviewer asked for sign-off 10, ACs unproven (`forge job acs`) 9, protocol break 5, schema without bump 4, devDependencies 3, version bump 2. About 40 of 56 name at least one fact the CLI already has (a protected path, `runbook.md` present, `acs` not all passing, a reviewer Summary containing ESCALATE, `package.json` or a version file in the diff); the `security` lens's `paths` list already encodes most of the "permissions class". Genuinely judgement-only: protocol breaks and "specific doubt", six to eight cases, which the approver got right. It also writes `pr.md`, the PR body, in both outcomes.

**Outside evidence.** No production system found uses an LLM to decide open-vs-escalate; Anthropic's policy is risk tiers (low: none once automated review and security checks are green; medium: one person; high: two), "an agent's approval never counts as a person's", and an approval covers one commit (vendor, [claude.dev](https://claude.dev/blog/solving-code-review-at-anthropic/)). Copilot's review can never approve or block a merge ([DEV](https://dev.to/ugliai/copilots-code-review-cannot-block-your-merge-it-says-so-in-the-docs-hfm)). Commentary on a same-vendor review study recommends a different-vendor reviewer for auth, payments and schema migrations ([BERI](https://www.beri.net/article/ai-to-ai-code-review-same-vendor-reviewer-independence-gap)).

**Inference.** The approver's value is as a classifier and PR author; the decision rule should be policy the CLI applies, so the park is auditable and cannot be argued past by reviewer prose. `policy.protectedPaths` (PP1–PP2) already does this for paths; `approver.alwaysEscalate` is the same policy as prose.

**Recommendation.** Add a deterministic `forge job gate` step before `decide`: it parks on protected paths, runbook presence, failing `acs`, reviewer ESCALATE summaries, manifest and version-file changes, and a `Size: L` with any blocker in the final round; it records the reasons as structured data. Spawn the approver only when no rule fired *and* `merge.auto` is on, or when the escalation needs prose; move `pr.md` authoring to sonnet (or the builder's final round). **Expected effect:** ~$50 of agent spend, but the real gain is gate noise: 56 escalation parks fall to roughly 10–15 that need a human's judgement, each with a machine-readable reason the dashboard's Needs-you card can show first. **How to measure:** `escalationRate` and the share of escalations later approved with no rework (today 47/50); if that share stays above ~80% the rules are still too wide. Evidence strength: moderate (consistent vendor policy, no measurement of LLM approver accuracy anywhere).

## Question 2: three orchestrator lanes run one relay prompt

**Forge today.** `forge dash` owns a pool (`lib/daemon-pool.ts`) of headless `claude -p --input-format stream-json` children, each supervised by one `Session` (`lib/daemon.ts`). Every child gets its argv from `daemon.command` and differs only in its session uuid, `FORGE_DAEMON_SCOPE` and a per-scope permission mode and effort (`buildArgv`, `lib/daemon.ts:461-474`; scope env at `:1258`): `_inbox`, `_office`, or a job id (`lib/requests.ts:20-22`). Each receives one stdin line, `/forge requests` (`TURN`, `lib/daemon.ts:190-194`), so **inbox, office and job sessions are the same orchestrator skill in three lanes**, told apart by `forge requests scope`. Work travels as request files in `.forge/runtime/requests/` (436 so far: resume 209, approve 157, gate 22, rework 18, sync 12); `requestScope()` routes `gate` to `_office`, a job request with a `job.json` to that job, everything else (`next`, `run`, `sync`, `retro`, `eval-run`) to `_inbox` (`lib/requests.ts:42-46`).

The **inbox** is two things sharing a name: the request queue (a file drop; "nothing here starts a process", `lib/requests.ts:1-4`) and the `_inbox` session, spawned eagerly at every pool start (`inboxEager`, `lib/daemon-pool.ts:94,257-260`) and never counted against `maxConcurrent`. Its turn is intake only: claim, run readiness triage for `next` (spawn `forge:triage`, haiku), `forge next --claim` or `forge job from-ref`, then `forge requests add resume --job <id>` and end. It is refused from driving a pipeline (`refuseInboxDrive`, `lib/daemon-owner.ts:112`, called at `lib/commands/advance.ts:64`). In a week of logs: 64 spawns (most are eager starts followed by a 30-minute idle stop), 18 turns, 0 failures, one non-triage agent ever (a retro, before the scope rule), $7.87 of thread cost and 13.3M cache-read tokens.

The **office** is the `_office` session, spawned on demand only while `daemon.enabled` and `foreman.enabled` (`lib/daemon-pool.ts:265-269`), shipped in 0.17.0 (PR #216). The **foreman** is the glossary's name for that session ("the office session that decides gates for jobs under `foreman.decides`", `docs/GLOSSARY.md:92`). It is an LLM session, not code: `lib/foreman.ts` is the *authority layer* around it (routing `maybeEnqueueGate :233`, the reserved-topic filter `:105-200`, `checkForemanDecision :277-309` which accepts `--actor foreman` only in the office scope, `OFFICE_JOB_COMMANDS :323`). Per `gate` request the office does `forge job assist` → spawn one **assistant** (`agents/assistant.md`, no Bash or Edit, opus here) with a paths-only brief → map its one-line `ASSIST:` verdict through a seven-row table (`reference/foreman.md:1-26`) to `forge job human … --actor foreman` and `forge job set` → `requests add resume` → ack. The office itself must never read a job file; it exercises no judgement, the assistant does. The "Office" of issue #281 (a director reading mandates, assistant foremen taking gates, `forge mandate`) is **not built**; nothing in `lib/` mentions a mandate.

In practice the office has decided nothing: `foremanDecisions: 0` in `forge stats`. The 22 `gate` requests ended as 4 stale (a human acted first), 14 rejected (4 by the reserved-topic filter before any spawn, 10 by the assistant escalating, 8 of them on a reserved topic: "decides a credentials/security posture", "changes who may approve irreversible publishing", and 2 over missing job files), and 4 done by the user's own interactive session standing in as foreman (memory `acting-as-foreman.md`). Office log: 9 spawns, 20 turns, 31 results of which 3 failed on expired OAuth, 11 assistant spawns, $8.98 thread cost. That is a policy fact (`foreman.reserved` and `approver.alwaysEscalate` cover most engine work), not a design fault, but it means the office has bought nothing yet.

The **per-job session** is where the cost is. 83 job sessions: 210 spawns, 390 supervisor turns plus **267 self-started wake turns** to record one agent end each, 718 results of which 50 were empty (`num_turns: 0`), **$217.95 of thread-only cost** against $1,888 of agent spend (~10%), 511M cache-read tokens; a resumed turn re-reads 57k–197k tokens of transcript per API call. Time is the larger overhead: 100 idle stops (65 to free a slot), a hand-off gap of median 82 s and p90 315 s per hop (~10 minutes per job of the 103-minute median), two 3.75-hour stalls from the pool-eviction bug (#120), and the missed-agent-end stall class fixed by a 60-second nudge in 0.17.0. Job 274 is a clean instance: one session, 15 results, 10 agent spawns, $11.63 session-reported of which the orchestrator thread was ~$1.4–1.7 (64 API calls, 5.6M cache-read tokens, 3k output tokens). What the LLM is left to do (`SKILL.md:48-60`): translate `spawn` into an Agent call, read the agent's first line and run `forge job agent end --import`, follow `procedure` references, map a dashboard request to a `forge job human` row (`reference/requests.md`), report one line per event. The decisions are in `step()` (94 rule ids, pure, "the one decider of pipeline transitions", `lib/step.ts:1-8`); the work is in the sub-agents. Evidence that the relay still costs judgement: 1,799 of 4,234 top-level Bash calls are compound despite the one-command rule; job 274 parsed `advance` output through `/tmp` and `node -e`; a session refused a CLI spec approval as "self-approval" (memory `overnight-orchestration-limits.md`).

**Why they are separate (stated reasons).** Blast radius and per-job logs: one crashed session used to stall every job (PR #54, `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md:228`); the inbox is the residue of the original single daemon, needed because a `next` has no job yet. Provenance for authority: `--actor foreman` is accepted only under `FORGE_DAEMON_SCOPE=_office`, which ARCHITECTURE.md:294 itself calls a boundary against a *misled* model, not a hostile process. Context isolation: the office never reads job files, job sessions resume their own transcripts. Cap bookkeeping: inbox and office are never counted against `maxConcurrent` or budget-held. None of these is about permissions: all three kinds of child get the same `DENY_TOOLS` and trust snapshot, and their argv differs only in the per-scope permission mode and effort.

**Outside evidence.** Anthropic's baseline is "workflows" (LLMs orchestrated through predefined code paths) first, agents only when simpler solutions fall short, and it names coding as a poor fit for multi-agent orchestration because tasks share context and rarely parallelise ([Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents); [multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Cognition: writes stay single-threaded, most subagents in practice are read-only and "mostly resemble tool calls"; "running multiple agents in collaboration only results in fragile systems" ([Don't Build Multi-Agents](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents); [What's Actually Working](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)). 12-factor agents: own your control flow, break the loop and wait for a human on high-stakes steps, make the agent a stateless reducer; good agents are "mostly just software" ([12-factor agents](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents); [factor 8](https://github.com/humanlayer/12-factor-agents/blob/HEAD/content/factor-08-own-your-control-flow.md)). The mainstream coding loop is the Ralph loop: a fixed prompt, fresh context per iteration, code restarts it, now at Assess on Thoughtworks' Radar with "coding agent swarms" at Caution ([Thoughtworks Radar](https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-de/radar/techniques/ralph-loop)). Intake in every shipped product is a deterministic trigger (Linear delegation, Devin `!triage` labels, Copilot automations, OpenHands `fix-me` label) with the human as admission authority ([Linear developers](https://linear.app/developers/agents); [Devin docs](https://docs.devin.ai/integrations/linear.md); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/coding-agent/about-coding-agent)). Products run one short-lived session per task (Copilot caps at 59 minutes) and no standing supervisor. On gates, a classifier caught 89% of injected dangerous commands against humans' 13.6%, which supports a policy or classifier for high-volume low-stakes approvals, while the 39%-of-plans rejection figure says plan-level gates are where humans still contribute ([Anthropic, auto mode](https://claude.com/blog/auto-mode-default-in-claude-code)). A stateless harness in one empirical study became reproducible only once the plan artifact was schema-validated too ([arXiv 2608.26197](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26197)).

**Inference.** Only two things in Forge need a model: the role agents, and, if #281 is built, a director choosing what to start. Every benefit the three lanes are said to buy is a property of the pool and the CLI, not of having three conversations: per-job strikes, budget holds, logs and resume ids are already per-`Session` objects keyed by scope; the authority line is enforced in `lib/foreman.ts` and would be *stronger* if the caller were the pool process than a model holding an env var; context isolation is moot for a code loop. The office, in particular, automates the gate where the human signal is strongest and has so far delegated every real decision back to a human. An LLM session that wraps a pure `step()` adds cost and drift without adding a decision; that is this audit's reading, not a claim from any one source.

**Recommendation**, in three steps that can land independently. First, **inbox → pool code**: on `next`/`run` the pool runs `forge next --claim` / `from-ref` in-process and spawns `forge:triage` as a one-shot `claude -p` child with `agents/triage.md`; drop `inboxEager`. Loses nothing observable; saves 64 spawns and ~13M cache-read tokens a week, 1–3 minutes per intake, and removes one place where tracker text meets an orchestrator context. Second, **office → `forge job assist --apply`**: the pool spawns the assistant headless, parses `ASSIST:`, and runs the same `forge job human --actor foreman` path through `checkForemanDecision` unchanged; the provenance becomes "the pool called it". Narrow the assistant to what the HITL evidence supports: decide only gates a rule marks low-risk (`rounds-exhausted` with no blocker, `conflicts` docs-only, `pr-feedback` from CI), and for `spec-approval` and `escalation` *draft a recommendation* the Needs-you card shows first, recording its override rate. Third, **per-job session → the pool loops `loadStepInput → step()`** (what `advance` does) and on a `spawn` launches `claude -p` with `agents/<role>.md` as system prompt and the brief as the message, in the job's worktree, with the same `allowedTools`, `DENY_TOOLS`, trust digest and `--permission-prompt-tool stdio`, recording `agent end` from the child's `result`. Keep `/forge` (SKILL.md) for a human at a terminal; it speaks the same `step` contract, so `docs/PROTOCOL.md`'s portability holds. The #281 director, if built, is one more one-shot child over a compact survey, not a resident conversation. **Expected effect:** the ~10% orchestrator thread spend (~$235 in this sample), the 267 wake turns, the empty-result and missed-agent-end classes, the "session improvises shell" class and the "self-approval" refusals go away; dashboard decisions apply in one tick instead of a $0.3–2.7 model turn (the server already calls engine code directly for `POST /api/jobs` and `/api/config`, so no new trust model); the hand-off gap (~10 min/job) shrinks to process start time. What it costs: a role-spawner in `lib/` (the `startShell` + Agent-tool glue the skill does today), a Thread view fed from child transcripts, and care with `can_use_tool` prompts from many short-lived children. What it loses: the Agent tool's `SendMessage` to a live agent and `--import` from subagent transcripts (the child's `result` line gives usage instead), and a chat-shaped per-job thread a human can read. **How to measure:** first add an `orchestrator` role to `forge stats` (import the session's `modelUsage` from its `result` records; the current estimate is 6–40% depending on single- vs multi-session jobs and is the biggest instrumentation gap), then compare minutes-to-PR, hand-off gap and `estCostUsd` per job before and after each step. Evidence strength: strong on principle (every primary source), absent on head-to-head measurement; the sizing (10%, 10 minutes) is Forge's own data.

## Question 3: 149 keys, about 36 of them necessary

**Forge today.** `defaults()` (`lib/config.ts:19-261`) has **149 leaf keys** plus 28 sub-keys on list elements; 51 default to `null`. This repo's `.forge/config.yml` is 266 lines and about 106 leaf keys, of which **about 34 differ from the default and 72 are written at their default** because `forge init write` serialises the merged config (`lib/commands/init.ts:90-96`). Ten keys are never read by the engine (`project.description/kinds/notes`, `infra.notes`, `runtime.provider/host`, `usage.track`, `release.versioning`, and two read only by prompts, `runtime.maxParallelJobs` and `improve.maxLearnings`); about 30 are read in exactly one place. Documented keys went from **81 to 140 value lines (+73%) in seven days**, schema from 1 to 20 with 15 bumps in the last four days (11 of them, v10–v20, for the multi-service work), all but five of the 19 migrations identity migrations whose only purpose is to make older Forges refuse the file (`lib/schema.ts:119-201`); `DEPRECATED_CONFIG_KEYS` is empty (`lib/deprecations.ts:19`), so nothing has ever been removed, and this repo's own file is still `schemaVersion: 3`, migrated in memory on every read. Duplication is systemic: `commands.*` restate `package.json` scripts and AGENTS.md's verify list; `worktrees.setup` equals `commands.setup`; `.github/workflows/**` is both a protected path (CLI-enforced) and an `alwaysEscalate` phrase (prompt); `review.panel[].when` (prose) and `paths` (globs) scope the same lens twice; `runtime.maxParallelJobs` and `daemon.maxConcurrent` cap the same resource (set to 2 and 4 here); `daemon.command` stores one flag (`--model sonnet`) as a 181-char command string beside `runtime.models.orchestrator`. The multi-service machinery (`components[].dependsOn/link/config`, `repos`, `checkout`, `backing`, `downstream`, `release.*`, `ports` ranges) is ≈45 sub-keys, 11 schema bumps and about two-fifths of `docs/CONFIG.md` (lines 561–935) that **no real job, template or eval has exercised**; this repo's five `components` all have `path: .` and feed only the dashboard's graph and the planner's `Services:` header (one job ever named one). 88 keys reach the dashboard (`lib/config-fields.ts`), 57 editable and 31 locked with a reason, which is the right pattern.

**Outside evidence.** Every coding agent now splits structured config (what a machine enforces or wires: permissions, hooks, sandbox, MCP, model, setup steps) from markdown (what a model interprets), and says outright that markdown is not enforcement ("an instruction like 'never edit .env' in CLAUDE.md or a skill is a request, not a guarantee", [Extend Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/features-overview); [Settings](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/settings); [Memory](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/memory)). Per-directory scoping is done by file placement or glob frontmatter, never a central component table, and project settings are deliberately *not* inherited across directories ([monorepo guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/large-codebases); [Codex AGENTS.md](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/agents-md); [Copilot instructions](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/configure-custom-instructions/add-repository-instructions)). Build tools infer from what is on disk (Nx reads tool configs and makes `project.json` override-only, [Nx inferred tasks](https://21.nx.dev/concepts/inferred-tasks); Turborepo keys tasks by `package.json` script names, [Turborepo](https://turborepo.dev/docs/crafting-your-repository/configuring-tasks)) or name a preset (Renovate's minimal file is `{"extends": ["config:recommended"]}`, [Renovate presets](https://docs.renovatebot.com/config-presets/)). ESLint's maintainers regret keeping several overlapping override mechanisms, "every step made logical sense for where we were at the time" until the team was "afraid of touching" it, and moved to one ([ESLint part 1](https://eslint.org/blog/2022/08/new-config-system-part-1/)); Prettier refuses options because each one is a permanent debate surface ([Prettier](https://prettier.io/docs/option-philosophy)); Google's SRE workbook: "our ideal configuration is no configuration at all", convert mandatory questions to optional ones with safe defaults, "most users will use the default", and reject unknown keys ([SRE Workbook ch. 14](https://sre.google/workbook/configuration-design/); [ch. 15](https://sre.google/workbook/configuration-specifics/)). Hadlow's clock: structured config that grows branching becomes a worse programming language ([Configuration Complexity Clock](https://mikehadlow.blogspot.com/2012/05/configuration-complexity-clock.html)). Always-loaded instruction files have real cost too: AGENTS.md files were associated with ~29% lower median runtime and ~17% fewer output tokens in one observational study ([arXiv 2601.20404](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20404)), while another found context files raised inference cost by over 20%, with LLM-generated ones slightly lowering success and developer-written ones gaining about 4% ([arXiv 2602.11988](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988)), so moving prose out of YAML is not free either; it should go to on-demand files, not to the root AGENTS.md.

**Inference.** The test for each key is: would a wrong value be a bug the engine must catch, or a judgement the agent should make? Applied to the inventory: **36 keys stay structured** (gates and rounds, `review.panel` as a lens list with `paths`/`model`/`checklist`, `review.blockOn`, PR and merge policy, safety: `policy.protectedPaths`, `verifySecrets`, `infra.mode/allowed`, `foreman.*`, `daemon.enabled/allowedTools/auth`, tracker policy, repo budgets, models and effort); **24 derive** with an optional override (`git.*`, `project.name/instructions`, `tracker.github.repo/project.*`, `commands.*` from manifests, `verify` as the non-null commands in order, `worktrees.setup/copy/cargo/generated`, `ports`, `dashboard.port`, `daemon.command`); **≈45 move into a preset** (`extends: forge/node-app`: the lens catalogue with its `paths`, `verifyE2e.paths`, protected-path defaults, a model profile, `foreman.reserved` and `daemon.allowedTools` default lists, retention); **10 become prose** in files the engine already hands to agents (`project.notes/description`, `infra.notes`, `components[].notes` → AGENTS.md; `approver.alwaysEscalate`, `merge.autoWhen`, `foreman.reserved` → `.forge/policy.md`; `review.panel[].when` → the lens checklist's first line); **14 are deleted** (the dead keys, the duplicate caps and aliases, `tracker.github.project.owner/number/statusField/statusMap`, `release.versioning/cadence`, `budget.tokensPerMonth`, `pipeline.budget.{tokens,minutes,changedLines}`, `components[].owners`). The multi-service keys are **deferred, not deleted**: they stay in `lib/` behind a "Multi-repo and services" page and out of `defaults()` output so a single-service repo never sees them, and when they come back, components should be detected from workspace manifests (`package.json#workspaces`, `Cargo.toml [workspace].members`, `go.work`) with per-directory markdown for guidance and the central table reserved for exceptions, which is the Turborepo/Nx/Claude Code shape and the only one that avoids ESLint's three-mechanism regret. The memory's multi-repo direction (coordinated releases, pluggable config) is not contradicted by this; it argues for *where* those keys surface, not whether they exist.

**Recommendation.** Mechanics, in order: `forge init write` serialises only values that differ from the preset-merged defaults; a `presets/<name>.yml` layer between `defaults()` and the file (`deepMerge(base, preset, file)` at `lib/config.ts:357`) with `extends` validated like `tracker.kind`; an append form for list keys (`daemon.allowedTools: [+git status]`) since `deepMerge` replaces arrays and that is why this repo copies the whole 15-entry default to add four subcommands; one migration (21) that folds aliases, drops dead keys, moves non-default prose into `.forge/policy.md` and strips defaults, finally using `DEPRECATED_CONFIG_KEYS`; `docs/CONFIG.md` split into "the ten keys you set", "presets" and "advanced". **Expected effect:** this repo's file from 266 lines / ~106 keys to about **35 lines / 14 keys**; a typical single-service web app on GitHub to **6–10 lines** (`extends`, tracker, `pr.conflicts`); `docs/CONFIG.md` front page from 953 lines to a tenth. **How to measure:** line count of `forge init` output on fixture repos (node app, cargo workspace, this repo); `forge doctor` unknown-key and dropped-entry reports; `test/config-set.test.ts` keeping the allowlist equal to `defaults()`. Evidence strength: strong (every vendor and every mature tool converged on the same split).

## Other optimisations the evidence supports

**Instrument before cutting.** The largest unknown in this audit is orchestrator spend: 6–40% on top of agent cost depending on how multi-session jobs are counted, and `forge stats` has no `orchestrator` role. Add it, fix `stats.reviewers.<lens>.blockers` (it reports 0 for every lens although the review files hold 10 `[blocker]` findings), add stage wall-clock to normal stats (only `eval compare` has it), record which lens's finding forced a round and whether a round-2+ finding was new or unresolved, and tag `human` events with the actor kind. None of this changes behaviour and all of it decides the arguments above.

**Run the A/Bs that already exist.** `forge eval start --set` can overlay `runtime.models.<role>` and the `runtime.effort*` keys, the editable `pipeline.*` keys, `review.panel` and `verify` (`lib/eval-overlay.ts:30`); one eval run has ever been recorded (2026-10-14, 4 tasks, `checkPassRate` 0.25 from a harness bug since fixed) and the six current tasks under `.forge/evals/` are untracked. The literature has no frontier-model, per-role ablation on real repos, so a repo-local A/B of `pipeline.plan=never`, `pipeline.maxRounds=2` and a warn-only `docs` lens is worth more than any paper cited here. Commit the tasks and run three overlays before changing a default.

**Make the builder's recurring failures a verifier, not a reviewer.** The 10-13 retro and `.forge/notes/builder.md` name the security hole families that drive half the extra rounds; whether the builder addendum moved `reviewers.security.changeRate` (0.344 → 0.262) is the open experiment in `.forge/notes/experiments.md`. A repo-local verifier at ~$0.01 per run is the cheapest executable feedback Forge can add, and executable feedback is the one stage every positive ablation keeps (AgentForge without its tester: 42% → 28%, [arXiv 2604.13120](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13120v1); surrogate-evaluated best-of-N selection 55.0 → 68.2, [arXiv 2602.03419](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.03419)).

**Use the office's authority layer, drop its conversation.** `lib/foreman.ts` is well designed: the gate must be in `decides`, not class-reserved, the action must belong to the gate, `pr-feedback` is keyed by head sha, and a non-PASS security verdict keeps any gate with a human. All of that survives the office session's removal unchanged.

**Calibrate the design flag.** 41 specs asked for design under `pipeline.design: none`; if the designer is never going to run here, the spec-writer's `Needs design:` rubric should say what the repo's config says, or the SP4 rule should read `pipeline.design` before the spec-writer is asked.

**Mind what moving prose costs.** Shifting `approver.alwaysEscalate`, `foreman.reserved` and lens `when` text into markdown files only pays if those files load on demand (the approver brief, the lens checklist), not into the root AGENTS.md that every agent reads; the AGENTS.md studies cut both ways.

## Prioritised changes, highest expected value per effort first

1. **Instrument the orchestrator**: an `orchestrator` role in `forge stats` from session `result.modelUsage`, stage wall-clock, sole-CHANGES lens, new-vs-unresolved finding, `actor.kind` on `human` events. Small change, settles the 6–40% question and every later measurement.
2. **Rework rounds re-run only CHANGES lenses plus `always`, diff since last round, no re-raised findings; `pipeline.maxRounds: 2`.** Expected 5–7% of spend and earlier escalation of stuck jobs; `step.ts` RV9/B2 rule changes plus brief text. A/B with `--set pipeline.maxRounds=2`.
3. **Per-lens `blocking: false`; set it on `docs` and `design`; `design` on sonnet unless `Needs design: yes`.** Removes the ~10 rounds those lenses alone forced (~$75) and ~$25 of opus review per 45 jobs; one config field, one `job verdicts` rule.
4. **`forge job gate`: deterministic escalation before `decide`; approver only for the residue; `pr.md` on sonnet.** ~$50 of spend, but 56 → ~15 escalation parks with structured reasons; builds on PP1–PP2.
5. **Inbox → pool code with a one-shot triage child; drop `inboxEager`.** Zero observable loss, 64 spawns and ~13M cache-read tokens a week, 1–3 minutes per intake.
6. **Commit the six eval tasks and run the three A/Bs** (plan=never, maxRounds=2, warn-only docs) before changing defaults.
7. **Fold the planner into the spec-writer** (`PLAN:` in the spec header, split gate at the spec stage). ~3–4% of spend and one role fewer; moderate `step.ts` work (SP5 must distinguish two parks).
8. **`pipeline.specApproval: large` by default plus rule-triggered gating for S/M specs; keep the note channel.** ~100 fewer parks per 125 jobs; measure person-only revise rate once `actor.kind` exists.
9. **A security verifier under `.forge/verifiers/` for the recurring hole families.** ~$0.01 per run, catches a share of the 33 security-only rounds before the panel.
10. **Office session → `forge job assist --apply` run by the pool; the assistant decides only rule-marked low-risk gates and drafts recommendations for spec-approval and escalation.** `lib/foreman.ts` unchanged; removes a lane that has decided nothing in 22 gates.
11. **Config shrink**: init writes deltas only, `extends:` presets, list-append form, migration 21 with `DEPRECATED_CONFIG_KEYS`, CONFIG.md split. 266 → ~35 lines here, 6–10 for a typical repo. Largest editing effort; mostly mechanical.
12. **Per-job orchestrator sessions → the pool loops `step()` and spawns role children headless.** The largest change and the largest payoff (~10% spend, ~10 min/job, four reliability classes), but it needs a role-spawner, a Thread view from child transcripts and `can_use_tool` handling; land it after 1, 5 and 10 have proven the pattern on the cheap lanes.

## What the evidence says to keep as is

`step()` as the pure decider and the CLI as the only writer of state (`lib/step.ts:1-8`, `SKILL.md:17`): this is 12-factor's "own your control flow" and the harness-engineering consensus, and it is what makes everything above a code change rather than a prompt change. Verify by execution before any model reads the diff, including the test-integrity report and secret scan: the one stage every vendor and every positive ablation keeps. The review panel's shape: parallel, fresh-context reviewers reading the diff, merged deterministically by severity tags against `review.blockOn`, with `security` on opus and path-scoped; it matches Devin Review's clean-context finding and SWR-Bench's merge result, and the lens pays for itself. The fresh builder per rework round (B4) with findings-first briefs: the Ralph-loop principle applied where it matters. The human gate on the PR with `merge.auto` off and `Auto-merge eligible: no` on 126 decisions: every vendor keeps a human approver, and Anthropic's "an agent's approval never counts as a person's" is the stated norm. `policy.protectedPaths`, the reserved-topic filter and `checkForemanDecision`: policy in code, which is where the HITL and merge-gate literature wants it. Worktrees outside the repo and the request files as the human↔pool interface: both survive every restructuring proposed here. The locked-with-reason config fields in the dashboard: the same "show the key, the source and the reason" pattern Claude Code uses for managed settings. And the glossary discipline itself, which made this audit's three Forge notes and four external notes speak the same language.

## Conclusion

The question that opened this audit, whether Forge is too thorough, has a sharper answer than yes or no: Forge spends its judgement well and its *relay* badly. The stages that embody a decision (spec, security review, verify, the authority checks in `lib/foreman.ts`) are the ones both the job data and the outside literature endorse; the cost sits in machinery that carries decisions already made, whether that is a panel re-confirming a pass in round three, an approver re-deriving a path rule, a planner whose verdict the spec-writer restates, or three LLM lanes reading `forge job advance` back to the CLI that produced it. Forge has, unusually, already built the deterministic engine the field recommends; it just still wraps it in the conversations that the engine was built to replace.

Two things follow. First, the order of work is instrumentation, then rounds and gates, then the lanes, then config: each step makes the next one measurable, and Forge's own `stats` and `eval --set` are better instruments than any study cited here, because no study ablates a frontier-model role pipeline on a real repo. Second, the multi-service work of the last four days is not wrong, it is early: the evidence says to keep that machinery behind a preset and a manifest-driven detector until a job actually needs it, so that the first repo to adopt Forge sees ten lines of config and a pipeline that ends a stuck job in two rounds instead of four.
